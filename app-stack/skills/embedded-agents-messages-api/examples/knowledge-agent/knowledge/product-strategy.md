# Product Strategy

## What it is

Product strategy is the **set of choices about where you will and won't compete, who you serve, and why you'll win**. It is not a roadmap, not a feature list, and not a vision statement. A useful strategy answers: *Given finite resources and an adversarial market, what specific bets are we making, and why are those bets defensible?*

Richard Rumelt's framing: a strategy has three components — a **diagnosis** of the challenge, a **guiding policy** for dealing with it, and a **coherent set of actions** that follow from the policy.

## The four questions a product strategy must answer

1. **Where will we play?** Which market, segment, geography, customer type. Equally important: where we will *not* play.
2. **How will we win?** What is the source of advantage — distribution, technology, network effects, brand, cost structure, regulatory moat? Pick one or two; you cannot win on all dimensions.
3. **What capabilities do we need?** Skills, data, infrastructure, partnerships required to deliver on the "how to win" claim.
4. **What management systems do we need?** Hiring, incentives, decision rights, metrics that reinforce the strategy rather than fight it.

A statement that doesn't make any of these four trade-offs sharper is a slogan, not a strategy.

## Strategy vs. vision vs. roadmap

- **Vision** — the long-term future state you want to create. Stable, qualitative, inspirational. (See `product-vision.md`.)
- **Strategy** — the bets you're making *now* to get there. Time-bound, contains explicit trade-offs.
- **Roadmap** — the sequence of concrete work that executes the strategy. Should be the *output* of strategy, not a substitute for it.

A common failure: jumping from vision straight to roadmap, skipping strategy entirely. The result is a roadmap that lists features without explaining why those features and not others.

## What makes a strategy "good"

Rumelt's test: **a good strategy has a kernel** — diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions — and **says no to most things**. A strategy that requires doing everything is no strategy. If two competing teams could read your strategy and both legitimately claim their work fits, the strategy is too vague.

Concretely, a good product strategy:

- **Names the constraint.** What's the central obstacle — distribution, retention, willingness to pay, talent? The strategy should be designed around relaxing that one constraint.
- **Picks a wedge.** Where in the market are you concentrating force to break in? A small, defensible beachhead beats a broad, undefended frontier.
- **States the source of advantage** in a sentence. "We win because of X" — where X is something a competitor would have to fundamentally restructure to replicate.
- **Implies what you will sacrifice.** Markets you won't serve, customers you'll lose, features you won't build. If nothing is being given up, no real choice has been made.

## Common failure modes

- **Strategy as goals.** "Become the leader in X" is a goal, not a strategy. The strategy is *how* you become the leader.
- **Strategy as values.** "Customer obsession" is a value. It is not a basis for choosing between two competing roadmaps.
- **Strategy by addition.** Adding "and also AI" to last year's strategy is not a strategy update. The question is what gets removed.
- **Strategy as PowerPoint.** A 60-slide deck that the team can't recite in two sentences won't change behavior. The strategy has to be small enough to live in working memory.

## How strategy interacts with execution

A strategy is a hypothesis. Execution is how you test it. Metrics — especially leading indicators — tell you whether the bets are paying off. When they're not, the strategy itself should be revisited, not just the tactics. Teams that adjust tactics endlessly while protecting a failing strategy are the most common form of organizational drift.
